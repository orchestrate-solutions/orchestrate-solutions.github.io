Coding Without Typing:
My Actual Workflow

The constraint forced the solution. Here’s what my development setup actually looks like.

I have bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome and bilateral cubital tunnel syndrome — both arms, both conditions. Sustained typing is painful. Long mouse sessions are painful. The kind of work software development normally requires is, for me, a rationed resource.

I still write software every day. This is how.

The controller

My primary pointing and navigation device is a game controller. I use it mapped to common IDE actions, navigation, text manipulation, window management — anything that would otherwise require sustained mouse or keyboard use. The controller distributes the physical load differently than a keyboard, and for my specific conditions it’s significantly more comfortable for extended sessions.

The Cross-Platform Controller Mapper — which I built specifically for this — handles the mapping. I have profiles for different contexts: development, browsing, design review. Switching profiles is a single button combination.

I’m not the first developer to use alternative input devices. But the tooling for this on macOS was genuinely inadequate when I started. That’s why I built my own.

Voice dictation

I use voice dictation for prose-heavy work — writing, documentation, commit messages, comments. macOS dictation has improved substantially and handles technical vocabulary better than it used to. For pure code, voice-to-code tools have gotten more capable, but I use them selectively.

The practical limit of voice dictation for programming isn’t the recognition quality — it’s the cognitive cost of translating intent into spoken syntax. Voice works well where you’re expressing ideas. It works less well where you’re doing symbol-dense work with precise indentation requirements.

AI augmentation

This is the piece that changed everything. I’ve spent four years building codeupipe — a Payload/Filter/Pipeline framework that forms the backbone of how I write and orchestrate software. It’s also the infrastructure that makes AI-augmented development actually work in practice rather than in theory.

The core insight from four years of doing this: AI doesn’t replace the developer, it shifts where the developer’s attention goes. I spend less time on typing and syntax, more time on architecture, judgment, and review. The quality of my decisions has gone up because I’m not burning cognitive capacity on mechanical work.

The constraint — not being able to type freely — forced me to build a workflow where the typing is a small fraction of the work. I don’t think that’s worse. I think it’s better. It just took a painful diagnosis to get me there.

The sessions themselves

I work in shorter bursts than most developers. A session might be 45-90 minutes before I need a break. I’ve learned to front-load judgment-heavy work — architecture decisions, code review, design — when I’m fresh, and let AI handle more of the mechanical output when I’m not.

The rest disciplines are non-negotiable. Not rest as productivity optimization — rest as actual physical requirement. Ignoring them has consequences I’ve learned the hard way.

What this actually produces

codeupipe: 47,000 lines, 5,000+ tests, four years of continuous development. Built almost entirely through this workflow. A controller, voice dictation, AI augmentation, and judgment-heavy human review.

I’m not sharing this as a model for everyone — my constraints are specific and most developers don’t have them. But if you have RSI, tendinitis, carpal tunnel, or any condition that makes sustained typing painful, I want you to know: you don’t have to stop. The workflow exists. You may have to build parts of it yourself. That’s okay.

The controller mapper is available on GitHub. The rest of this workflow is what I’m building when I write about accessibility-first development.
