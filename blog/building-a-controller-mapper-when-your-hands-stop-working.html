Building a Controller Mapper
When Your Hands Stop Working

I didn’t see a gap in the market. I hit a wall and had to build the door.

For most of my life, I interacted with computers the way everyone does — keyboard, mouse, take it for granted. I spent years in the Air Force, years at eBay, years building my own software. Typing was just how you did things. I never thought about it.

Then I got diagnosed with bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome and bilateral cubital tunnel syndrome. Both conditions, both arms. The kind of diagnosis that ends software careers.

Gaming, which I’d loved for years, became difficult. Not impossible — but playing for long stretches with keyboard and mouse stopped being painless. More pressingly: coding, which had been my livelihood and my craft, became something I had to ration. Every session had a cost.

The obvious solution that didn’t exist

Controllers are ergonomic in a way keyboards aren’t. They’re designed for extended use. The grip distributes load across your hands differently. For someone with carpal tunnel, a well-configured controller is genuinely more comfortable than a mouse for many tasks.

The problem: the tools that let you map a controller to keyboard and mouse actions were, frankly, a mess if you weren’t on Windows.

reWASD is the most capable tool in this space. It’s also Windows-only. I’m on macOS. I looked for alternatives. What existed was either abandoned, limited, or required a level of configuration complexity that defeated the purpose. I needed something that just worked — flexible enough to do what I needed, but not a second job to configure.

So I built it.

What “built it for myself” actually means

People say “I built this for myself” as a humble-brag sometimes. I mean it literally. The Cross-Platform Controller Mapper was not a side project I was excited about. It was infrastructure. I needed it to keep working.

The macOS implementation came first because that’s what I was using. I mapped controller inputs to the keyboard shortcuts I used most for coding. Navigation, common IDE actions, text editing. Then I extended it to handle gaming — and found that the same configuration that made development more accessible made gaming more comfortable too.

I’ve been using it daily for a long time. That’s the only real test that matters. If something breaks, I feel it immediately. If something is configured wrong, I know. The feedback loop is zero latency because I am the user.

Why I’m sharing it

There are a lot of people who interact with computers differently — by necessity or by choice. People with repetitive strain injuries, with motor conditions, with temporary injuries, with preferences that don’t fit the default keyboard-and-mouse assumption. And there are gamers who want reWASD-quality control on macOS or Linux and simply don’t have it.

I’m not sharing this because I want to run a software company. I’m sharing it because I built something that solves a real problem, it works, and other people have that same problem. If it helped me, it might help you.

The cross-platform roadmap — Linux, Windows, Android — is real. macOS is working. The others are in progress. I build when I have capacity. This is a solo project maintained by someone with limited hand function, so it moves at the pace it moves.

The thing I didn’t expect

Building this changed how I think about software. Not in a grand philosophical way — in a practical one. When you build for yourself first, your tolerance for unnecessary complexity drops to zero. When the tool has to work or you can’t do your job, reliability is not a nice-to-have. When the user is you, there’s no one to blame for bad design decisions but yourself.

Every tool in this repo has been through my hands. Or rather, through the accommodations I’ve built because my hands don’t cooperate the way they used to. That’s the only quality guarantee I can honestly offer — and I think it’s the best one there is.

The controller mapper lives on GitHub. If you’re on macOS and you’ve been looking for something like this, try it.
